It is incredibly common to look at SCL and SDA and immediately think I2C, but on these cheap 1.8" ST7735 TFT modules, those labels are a bit of a misnomer. Here is why it's definitely SPI and how those pins actually map out:

Why it's SPI (and how to decode the labels)
The dead giveaway is the presence of the CS (Chip Select) and DC (Data/Command) pins. Standard I2C displays don't use these, but they are absolutely essential for a 4-wire SPI display.

The manufacturer just reused standard I2C naming conventions for the clock and data lines. Here is the actual translation to standard SPI terminology:

```
SCL -> SPI SCK (Serial Clock)
SDA -> SPI MOSI (Serial Data In / Master Out Slave In)
RST -> Reset (Hardware reset pin)
DC  -> Data/Command select (Sometimes labeled as RS or A0)
CS  -> Chip Select (Active LOW to enable the display)
BLK -> Backlight control (Can be tied to VDD or driven via PWM to control brightness)
```

Driving a high-resolution display

Driving 128 x 160 color pixels requires pushing a massive amount of data compared to a tiny 128 x 64 monochrome OLED. If this were running over typical 400kHz I2C, the refresh rate would be painfully slow—essentially a slideshow. SPI allows you to crank the clock speed up to 15 to 30 MHz (or higher, depending on your microcontroller), giving you smooth rendering.

If you are setting this up with an ESP32-S3 using libraries like TFT_eSPI or Adafruit_ST7735, you will just map your chosen hardware SPI pins to SCL (Clock) and SDA (MOSI), and allocate three free GPIOs for RST, DC, and CS.
